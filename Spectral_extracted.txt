Abstract
We present a construction of a self-adjoint operator on a bipartite quantum graph
whose spectral-shift distribution equals, in the sense of tempered distributions, the
arithmetic von Mangoldt measure. Assuming the existence of a spectral realization
(Hilbert–P´olya conjecture), we show that the operator is uniquely determined by com-
patibility with the Euler product structure (arithmetic data) and the functional equa-
tion symmetry (analytic constraint).
The construction proceeds on a vesica-constrained star graph, where the geometric
constraint (vesica piscis with scale r = 2α) minimizes parametric entropy, and the
arithmetic constraint (arithmetic signature gα(n) = 2α/d(n) with singularity strength
α) encodes the prime distribution. The operator’s self-adjointness and discrete spec-
trum follow from the geometric confinement (vesica compactness) and the star-graph
topology (regularization of singular prime data). The spectral theorem then implies
that all eigenvalues are real, and the correspondence between eigenvalues and zeros
yields spectral rigidity for the critical line ℜ(s) = α.

1.1
The Hilbert–P´olya Conjecture and Spectral Realization
The Hilbert–P´olya program seeks to explain the distribution of zeros of L-functions by finding
a self-adjoint operator whose eigenvalues coincide with the zeros. This approach is motivated
by the observation that if such an operator exists, the spectral theorem would immediately
3

imply that all eigenvalues (zeros) are real, providing a natural explanation for the location
of zeros on the critical line.
The central question is: Given an L-function L(s) with Euler product and functional
equation, does there exist a self-adjoint operator ˆH whose spectrum matches the zeros of
L(s)? If such an operator exists, what is its unique form?
This paper addresses the inverse spectral problem: we assume that a spectral realization
exists (the Hilbert–P´olya conjecture) and construct an operator compatible with the arith-
metic and analytic data. We show that the operator constructed on a vesica-constrained star
graph reproduces the Euler product structure and respects the functional equation symmetry.
The self-adjointness and discrete spectrum of this operator then follow from its geometric
and arithmetic constraints.

Our framework follows the structure: First, we establish α as fundamental through both

approach explains why the specific line ℜ(s) = α is mathematically necessary.
Since the Euler product for an L-function encodes prime distribution and its nontrivial

of the zeros. Our framework develops these ideas and shows that the existence of any zero
off the line ℜ(s) = α leads to an unavoidable internal contradiction within the L-function’s
defining structure.
The location of zeros of L-functions and their relationship to prime distribution remain
significant open questions [3,4].
For over a century, the Guinand–Weil explicit formula, P h(γ) = [Geometric/Analytic Terms]+

zeros of an L-function and the prime numbers. The Hilbert–P´olya program was born from

find an unknown operator that fits the known data.

There is no “duality” and no “inverse problem” to be solved. There is only a definitional

are not arbitrary expressions; they are the necessary, minimal, and canonical geometric and
arithmetic representations of the L-function itself. The operator ˆH we construct is not a

Therefore, the explicit formula is not a statement A = B that requires a new proof of

represents the L-function’s geometric and arithmetic structure.
The spectral rigidity follows from the uniqueness of the operator: if a spectral realization
exists, it must be the vesica-constrained star graph operator, which is self-adjoint with
discrete spectrum. The spectral theorem then forces all eigenvalues to be real. This paper
identifies the unique operator compatible with the arithmetic and analytic constraints, and
demonstrates its spectral properties.
1.2
On the Nature of this Construction
This paper presents a construction for the inverse spectral problem for L-functions that differs
from conventional approaches. Rather than searching for an operator through computational
methods or asymptotic analysis, we construct an operator by requiring compatibility with
both the arithmetic data (Euler product) and the analytic constraint (functional equation).
1.3
The Inverse Spectral Approach
The conventional approach to the Hilbert–P´olya program has been to search for a self-
adjoint operator whose eigenvalues match the zeros, treating this as an open problem. This
approach has proven extraordinarily difficult, as there are many possible operators and no
clear criterion for selection.
Our approach is different. We assume the existence of a spectral realization (the Hilbert–
P´olya conjecture) and construct an operator compatible with the arithmetic and analytic
constraints.
We show that the vesica-constrained star graph construction is compatible
with both constraints, and its spectral properties follow from its geometric and arithmetic
structure.

1.5
The Weil Explicit Formula as Definitional Identity
The Guinand–Weil explicit formula is typically viewed as a profound but mysterious relation-
ship between the zeros of an L-function and prime distribution. Our framework reveals that
this formula is not a relationship to be explained but a definitional identity that expresses
the necessary consistency of the L-function’s three facets.
The left-hand side of the explicit formula represents the analytic aspect (the zeros), while
the right-hand side represents the geometric and arithmetic aspects. The equality is not a
coincidence or a deep theorem; it is the mathematical expression of the fact that these are
different representations of the same object.

B.1
Formal Definitions
Definition B.1 (Mohr-Mascheroni Construction). A Mohr-Mascheroni (Compass-
Only) Construction M is a finite sequence of steps M = (S1, S2, . . . , Sn).
Let P0 be
a set of initially given points in C (e.g., {0, 1} for scale). Let Pi be the set of all points
constructed up to step i, and Ri be the set of all distances (radii) defined by pairs of points
in Pi. Each step Sj (j ≥1) must be one of the following:
1. Draw Circle: Choose a center c ∈Pj−1 and a radius d ∈Rj−1. Draw the circle
Cj = {z ∈C : |z −c| = d}.
2. Mark Intersection: Choose two distinct circles Ca, Cb (a, b < j) that intersect. Mark
their intersection point(s) zint = Ca ∩Cb. The set of constructed points is updated:
